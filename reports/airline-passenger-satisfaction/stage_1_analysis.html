1. Executive Summary

This report investigates patterns in airline passenger satisfaction using demographic, travel, service-rating and flight-delay information. The objective is to identify passenger segments and aspects of the travel experience associated with satisfaction.

Key findings

  • Travel characteristics: Class and type of travel have the strongest categorical associations with satisfaction.
  • Service experience: Online boarding has the strongest association among the measured service ratings, followed by inflight entertainment, Wi-Fi and seat comfort.
  • Customer loyalty: Loyal customers have a substantially higher satisfaction rate than disloyal customers.
  • Flight delays: Delays are associated with satisfaction, but their effect sizes are relatively weak.
  • Gender: Despite statistical significance, gender has a negligible association with satisfaction.
Overall interpretation: Passenger satisfaction is more strongly associated with travel characteristics and reported service experience than with the occurrence or duration of flight delays. These are observational associations, not evidence of causal relationships.

2. Business Objectives and Research Questions

The analysis is guided by the following business question:

Which aspects of the passenger journey are most strongly associated with customer satisfaction, and how can passenger data help an airline identify dissatisfied customers and prioritize service improvements?

Research questions

  1. How is satisfaction distributed across the passenger population?
  2. How does satisfaction differ across customer and travel segments?
  3. Which service ratings are most strongly associated with satisfaction?
  4. How are flight delays related to passenger satisfaction?

Missing arrival delay values

The training dataset contains 310 missing values in Arrival Delay in Minutes, corresponding to approximately 0.30% of observations.

Passengers with missing arrival delay values have a higher average departure delay than the overall population: 37.43 minutes compared with 14.82 minutes. This suggests that missingness may be related to flight circumstances and should not automatically be treated as random.

The satisfaction distributions are relatively similar: 41.29% of passengers with missing arrival delay values are satisfied, compared with 43.34% among passengers with non-missing values.

Handling decision: Missing arrival delays should not be interpreted as zero-minute delays. Missingness should be preserved or explicitly accounted for during analysis and modelling.

Special values and data considerations

  • Inflight Wi-Fi: A rating of 0 represents "Not Applicable", not a dissatisfied rating. Wi-Fi comparisons therefore exclude zero values.
  • Other service ratings: Ratings are ordinal values, generally ranging from 1 to 5. Zero values should only be excluded if their meaning is established by the data documentation.
  • Flight delays: Delay distributions are strongly right-skewed. Extreme values should not automatically be removed, as they may represent genuine operational disruptions.
  • Identifier: The ID column is an identifier and should not be used as a predictive feature.
  • Categories: Inconsistent capitalization and whitespace should be normalized before further analysis.

6. Statistical Analysis

Hypothesis tests were conducted to assess whether the observed differences between satisfaction groups were statistically significant.

Chi-square tests were used to evaluate associations between categorical passenger characteristics and satisfaction. Cramér's V was calculated to assess association strength.

Class and type of travel have the largest effect sizes. Customer type has a smaller association, while delay occurrence has weak associations. Gender has a negligible effect despite its statistically significant result.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare service-rating distributions between satisfied and neutral/dissatisfied passengers. This non-parametric test is suitable for ordinal ratings and does not require normally distributed observations.

6.4 Multiple hypothesis testing

Holm correction was applied separately to the six categorical tests, fourteen service-rating tests and two delay-duration tests. This controls the family-wise error rate within each group of comparisons.

The adjusted results preserve the main findings: all categorical tests remain significant, all service comparisons except gate location remain significant, and both delay-duration comparisons remain significant.

7. Business Interpretation

7.1 Prioritize the digital passenger experience

Online boarding has the strongest association with satisfaction among the measured service variables. This suggests that the digital check-in and boarding journey deserves particular attention when investigating passenger experience.

7.2 Investigate the onboard experience

Inflight entertainment, Wi-Fi, seat comfort, legroom and onboard services show relatively strong associations. These areas could be examined further to identify specific service shortcomings and opportunities for improvement.

7.3 Segment customers before interpreting satisfaction

Satisfaction varies considerably across travel purpose and class. Comparing passengers within similar travel segments may provide a more useful understanding than examining overall averages alone.

7.4 Treat operational delays as one part of the experience

Delays are associated with lower satisfaction, but their effect sizes are relatively weak. Operational performance should still be monitored, while considering the broader passenger journey and service experience.

These findings are exploratory. They do not establish that changing a particular service will cause satisfaction to increase. Further adjusted analysis and business validation are necessary before prioritizing investments.

8. Limitations

  • Observational data: Associations cannot establish causal relationships.
  • Combined target classes: Neutral and dissatisfied passengers cannot be distinguished in the supplied target variable.
  • Potential confounding: Travel purpose and class are related, making independent interpretation difficult without multivariable analysis.
  • Survey-based ratings: Service ratings represent reported passenger experience and may be influenced by overall satisfaction itself.
  • Missing arrival delays: The 310 missing values may be related to operational circumstances and require careful handling.
  • Large sample size: Statistical significance can occur even when practical differences are small. Effect sizes should therefore guide interpretation.
  • Dataset applicability: The dataset is not established as representative of Finnair passengers. Findings should not be generalized directly to Finnair.

9. Next Steps

The exploratory findings provide a foundation for multivariable analysis and predictive modelling. The next stage will investigate whether the observed associations remain informative when multiple passenger characteristics are considered together.

  1. Multivariable analysis: Investigate the combined relationships between travel purpose, class, customer type and satisfaction.
  2. Baseline predictive model: Implement logistic regression to estimate passenger satisfaction using customer and flight characteristics.
  3. Model comparison: Compare the baseline with a tree-based model and evaluate precision, recall, F1-score and ROC-AUC.
  4. Feature interpretation: Identify which variables contribute most to predictive performance and investigate their relationships with satisfaction.
  5. Business intelligence: Translate the findings into a Power BI dashboard with passenger segments, satisfaction indicators and actionable recommendations.
Important modelling distinction: A model using only passenger and flight characteristics can explore prediction without survey ratings. A second model can include service ratings to assess their additional predictive information. The latter should not be presented as a pre-flight prediction model, because the ratings are collected after the passenger has experienced the service.

10. Conclusion

The exploratory and statistical analysis identifies substantial differences in passenger satisfaction across travel characteristics and reported service experiences.

Class and travel purpose exhibit the strongest categorical associations with satisfaction, while online boarding is the strongest service-rating association. Inflight entertainment, Wi-Fi and seat comfort also demonstrate meaningful differences between satisfaction groups.

Flight delays are statistically associated with satisfaction, but their effect sizes are relatively weak. Gender, despite statistical significance, shows negligible practical association.

Overall, the findings suggest that understanding passenger satisfaction requires considering the broader travel context and service experience, rather than focusing exclusively on operational disruptions.

These results establish a foundation for the next stage: developing predictive models that account for multiple passenger characteristics and translating the findings into business-oriented insights.
